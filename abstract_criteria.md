# Criteria for a Strong Biomedical Journal Abstract

1. Clear problem statement
   The opening should identify the specific clinical, scientific, or methodological gap. Avoid starting too broadly.

2. Specific objective
   State the study aim in one clear sentence so the reader knows exactly what question the article answers.

3. Appropriate structure
   Use the target journal's required format when available, commonly `Background`, `Objective`, `Methods`, `Results`, and `Conclusions`.

4. Methods with enough detail
   Include the study design, data source, population or sample, intervention or algorithm, comparator when relevant, and main outcomes.

5. Results, not promises
   Report actual findings with concrete numbers, such as sample size, effect sizes, sensitivity, specificity, confidence intervals, alert rates, or primary outcome values.

6. No overclaiming
   Ensure conclusions match the evidence. Do not claim error detection if the study only supports remeasurement alerting or prioritization.

7. Clinical or scientific relevance
   Explain why the findings matter for practice, research, data quality, workflow, or scientific understanding.

8. Terminology discipline
   Use key terms consistently and preserve important distinctions, such as `error` versus `remeasurement-worthy`, `outlier` versus `implausible value`, and `ground truth` versus `injected simulated error`.

9. Self-contained
   Make the abstract understandable without the full paper. Avoid unexplained acronyms, citations, figure references, or assumptions that require outside context.

10. Concise and polished
    Keep the abstract within the journal's word limit, usually about 250-350 words for biomedical journals, and make every sentence contribute directly to the argument.
