# Background

Accurate assessment of pediatric growth depends on repeated measurements of height, length, and weight, yet these measurements are inherently vulnerable to error. Anthropometric error can arise from imprecision in repeated measurement, systematic bias, equipment calibration, observer technique, child positioning and cooperation, diurnal variation, recording, transcription, and unit conversion [1,3]. The construction of major growth standards required unusually intensive quality-control procedures, including standardized equipment, duplicate independent measurements, preset remeasurement thresholds, repeated observer training, and ongoing monitoring across sites [2,3]. These procedures illustrate an important practical point: even in research settings designed around measurement quality, anthropometry is treated as an error-prone process that requires immediate verification when measurements differ beyond acceptable limits.

Electronic health records (EHRs) have made longitudinal growth data available at a scale that prospective research studies cannot usually match. These data are valuable for surveillance, clinical research, and learning health systems, but they are collected during routine care rather than under research protocols [4,7,11]. Pediatric EHR growth data therefore contain a mixture of true biological variation, clinically meaningful growth abnormalities, random measurement error, systematic measurement error, carried-forward values, duplicate observations, unit errors, switched height and weight fields, and typographical or transcription errors [7,11]. This mixture creates a central methodological challenge: once a measurement has been entered and the child has left the clinical encounter, the true value at that age is generally unrecoverable.

Traditional approaches to growth data quality often begin by identifying biologically implausible values using fixed population cutoffs or growth-chart z-score thresholds. These rules are useful for detecting extreme impossible or near-impossible values, but they can conflate two distinct concepts: outlying values and implausible values [8]. A plausible outlier may represent a real child at the tail of the population distribution, whereas an implausible inlier may fall within a normal population range while being inconsistent with that child's own trajectory [8]. This distinction is especially important in pediatrics, where a value can be population-plausible but trajectory-discordant, such as a sudden height decrease or a large isolated jump that returns to the prior growth channel.

Evidence from large pediatric datasets shows that population-based biologically implausible value (BIV) thresholds can remove true extreme values. In NHANES 1999-2012, most high weight, height, and BMI values flagged by WHO-based BIV cutoffs were supported by other body-size measures, and excluding flagged values reduced estimated obesity prevalence [5]. In a subsequent analysis of 13.7 million observations from 8.8 million low-income children, high BIVs frequently recurred at follow-up, suggesting that many values classified as biologically implausible by standard cutoffs were unlikely to be simple errors [6]. More broadly, a comparison of 11 BIV methods found that exclusion rates and severe-obesity prevalence estimates varied across methods, and that many large epidemiologic studies did not fully report how BIVs were handled [4]. A recent systematic review similarly found heterogeneous approaches to anthropometric data-quality assessment and recommended using multiple quality indicators rather than a single cutoff [13]. These findings argue against treating cross-sectional cutoffs as definitive error labels.

Longitudinal methods address some of these limitations by evaluating measurements in the context of an individual child's growth history. Conditional growth percentiles can identify values that are unlikely given a prior trajectory and may detect errors missed by cross-sectional screening [9]. Daymont and colleagues developed an automated pediatric EHR cleaning method that compares each measurement with weighted moving averages of the child's other measurements and separately handles duplicates, carried-forward values, unit errors, switched fields, sparse trajectories, and large height decreases [7]. Other approaches have used mixed-effects models, robust regression, local trajectory rules, or semi-parametric algorithms to identify implausible height and weight values in longitudinal pediatric records [10-12]. These methods support the general premise that within-child trajectory information improves detection of suspicious measurements.

However, longitudinal cleaning algorithms still face a fundamental limitation: retrospective labels are not ground truth. A trajectory-discordant value may be a data-entry error, a measurement error, normal biological fluctuation, pubertal timing, disease-related growth change, treatment response, or an early signal of an endocrine, nutritional, gastrointestinal, oncologic, or other condition. Sparse data increase uncertainty, and even expert manual review is partly judgment-based [7,9,12]. Simulation studies can provide known injected errors, but their conclusions depend on assumptions about the true growth process and the error-generation mechanism [12]. Clinician consensus review can provide a useful benchmark, but it should be interpreted as an operational judgment rather than direct observation of the true value.

The risk of over-cleaning is not only statistical but also clinical and equity-relevant. Removing unusual values can discard the very observations most relevant to severe obesity, failure to thrive, growth acceleration, growth faltering, or rare growth disorders [5,6,8,10]. Population-level norms may also remove data from groups whose physiology or social context differs from the reference population. Recent equity-focused work on EHR data cleaning recommends greater reliance on individual-level patterns and conservative exclusion of only clearly impossible values when population norms may not represent marginalized communities [14]. For pediatric growth, this supports treating unusual measurements as signals for verification or review, rather than silently deleting them whenever they deviate from a population reference.

These considerations motivate a prospective remeasurement-alert framing. Instead of asking whether an ambiguous historical measurement was truly erroneous, the clinically actionable question is whether a newly entered measurement is suspicious enough to warrant immediate repeat measurement while the child is still present. This framing aligns with how high-quality anthropometric protocols manage uncertainty: discordant duplicate measurements trigger remeasurement at the point of care [3]. It also avoids overclaiming retrospective truth. A prospective system can use only information available up to the current encounter, estimate how strongly the new value deviates from the child's expected trajectory, and produce an alert or score that allows clinics to choose thresholds based on tolerance for missed errors, false-positive remeasurements, and workflow burden.

Accordingly, the contribution of a trajectory-aware remeasurement alert system is not to adjudicate all historical growth values as true or false. Its value is to operationalize uncertainty at the moment when action is still possible. Evaluation should therefore report threshold-dependent tradeoffs, such as the proportion of injected errors captured in simulated data, the total alert rate, the number of non-injected measurements that would be remeasured, and the expected remeasurement workload for a typical clinic. This approach preserves the distinction between data cleaning and clinical verification, keeps plausible extreme growth visible, and reframes longitudinal growth modeling as a tool for improving measurement quality before ambiguity becomes irreversible.
